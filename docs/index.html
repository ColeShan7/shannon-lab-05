Physician Office Visits Among Older Adults

Author

Cole Shannon

Introduction

This report examines factors associated with the number of physician office visits among older adults in the National Medical Expenditure Survey. Because physician visits are nonnegative integer counts, we begin by exploring the distribution and then compare Gaussian and Poisson regression models. Later sections evaluate dispersion, negative binomial alternatives, excess zeros, and an interaction.

Part 1: Data Preparation and Exploration

1.1 Load packages and prepare the data

The outcome is the number of physician office visits (visits). The five predictors are number of chronic conditions (chronic), self-perceived health (health), gender (gender), years of education (school), and private insurance (insurance).

The reference groups are average health, male, and no private insurance.

The Gaussian model’s DHARMa uniformity test was significant (p < .001). More fundamentally, Gaussian regression treats physician visits as a continuous outcome, assumes a constant residual variance, and can produce predictions below zero. Because physician visits are nonnegative integer counts with substantial positive skew, a count regression model is more appropriate.

3.3 Interpret the diagnostics

The dispersion ratio for M2 was 7.25 (p < .001).

The significant ratio above one indicates substantial overdispersion: the outcome is more variable than the fitted Poisson model expects.

The observed-to-expected ratio of zeros was 15.03 (p < .001).

The model substantially underpredicts the observed number of zero visits.

The residual-uniformity test yielded p < .001.

Interpretation of the graphs

The Poisson Q-Q plot shows a pronounced S-shaped departure from the reference diagonal. The residual-versus-fitted plot also reveals concentrations of observations near zero and one rather than an approximately uniform distribution.

Together, these patterns suggest that the Poisson model does not reproduce the observed count distribution adequately. They are consistent with the significant dispersion, zero-inflation, and uniformity tests.

Why overdispersion matters

The Poisson model assumes that the conditional variance equals the conditional mean.

When the outcome is overdispersed, the model-based standard errors can be underestimated. This can produce confidence intervals that are too narrow and p-values that are overly optimistic, increasing the risk of incorrectly concluding that predictors are statistically significant.

Why we should not immediately fit a zero-inflated model

Excess zeros do not necessarily indicate a separate structural-zero process.

Overdispersion can itself cause a Poisson model to underestimate the number of zeros. A negative binomial model allows additional variance and may therefore address both overdispersion and excess zeros without requiring an explicit zero-inflation component.

We first estimate a negative binomial model and reassess its diagnostics before deciding whether a separate zero process is necessary.

Part 4: Negative Binomial Regression

4.1 Fit the NB2 model

The Poisson diagnostics indicated substantial overdispersion and many more zeros than predicted.

We therefore fit a negative binomial model (NB2), using the same five predictors and observations as M2.

The NB2 variance is Var(Y | X) = μ + μ²/θ.

The additional dispersion parameter permits the variance to exceed the mean.

Code 
nb2 <- glmmTMB(
  visits ~ chronic + health + gender + school + insurance,
  data = analysis_data,
  family = nbinom2(link = "log")
)

Why does the predicted line curve?

The line curves even though the model is linear on the log scale.

With a log link, a one-unit increase in chronic conditions produces a constant multiplicative change in expected visits rather than a constant additive change.

Back-transforming the log-linear predictor using the exponential function produces curvature on the count scale. This explains why predicted visits increase more rapidly as the number of chronic conditions increases.

Part 5: Do the Zeros Need Their Own Model?

5.1 Decide whether another zero model is justified

The NB2 model substantially improved the dispersion problem. Its dispersion ratio was 1.04 (p = 0.432).

However, its observed-to-expected zero ratio was 1.10 (p < .001).

The significant ratio above one indicates that NB2 still predicts too few zero visits, providing justification for comparing models with a separate zero component.

We consider two alternatives:

  • Zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB): Zeros can arise from either the count-generating process or a separate structural-zero process.
  • Hurdle negative binomial: A separate process models whether a person records any physician visits, while positive visit counts are modelled using a zero-truncated negative binomial distribution.

Both models include the same five predictors in their conditional count and zero components.

These models represent statistical processes and do not establish that particular individuals belong to a literal group that can never visit a physician.

  data = analysis_data,

)

  visits ~ chronic + health + gender + school + insurance,

That invariance does not automatically extend to contrasts of the full two-part hurdle-model predictions.

Paragraph 1 — Model selection

We analysed physician office visits among 4406 older adults (M = 5.77, variance = 45.69), of whom 15.5% reported no visits. The full Poisson model demonstrated substantial overdispersion (ratio = 7.25, p < .001) and underestimated zero counts (observed/expected ratio = 15.03, p < .001). NB2 significantly improved fit relative to Poisson, χ²(1) = 12,129.08, p < .001. NB2 addressed the dispersion mismatch (ratio = 1.04, p = 0.432), but still underestimated zeros (ratio = 1.10, p < .001), motivating comparison with models incorporating a separate zero component. Among the converged models, Hurdle NB had the lowest AIC (24,330.41) and BIC (24,426.27). Its zero ratio was 1.00 (p = 1.000), although significant dispersion (p < .001) and uniformity (p = 0.002) tests indicated remaining model misspecification.

Paragraph 2 — Main effects

Holding the other predictors constant, each additional chronic condition was associated with greater conditional count-component intensity (b = 0.148, 95% CI [0.123, 0.172], p < .001). Relative to average perceived health, poor health was associated with higher count intensity (b = 0.383, 95% CI [0.287, 0.478], p < .001), whereas excellent health was associated with lower count intensity (b = -0.368, 95% CI [-0.501, -0.236], p < .001). Female gender relative to male gender was not a statistically significant predictor (b = 0.056, 95% CI [-0.008, 0.121], p = 0.088). The reference groups were average health, male gender, and no private insurance. These estimates describe the conditional count component of the selected model, rather than the full expected number of visits after accounting for its zero component.

Paragraph 3 — Interaction

Adding the chronic-conditions × private-insurance interaction to the conditional count component did not significantly improve model fit, χ²(1) = 0.34, p = 0.559. The chronic-conditions slope was b = 0.160, 95% CI [0.111, 0.208], p < .001, among participants without private insurance, and b = 0.143, 95% CI [0.115, 0.172], p < .001, among those with private insurance. The largest absolute conditional insurance contrast occurred at −1 SD. Although insurance-group contrasts were detectable at the examined chronic-condition values, the nonsignificant interaction test does not establish that the chronic-conditions slopes differed between groups.

7.2 Discussion

The findings demonstrate why count regression requires careful attention to the distribution of the outcome and the assumptions of the fitted model. Physician visits exhibited considerable variability, and the initial Poisson model substantially underestimated both dispersion and zeros. NB2 improved the representation of variability, but it continued to underpredict zeros. The hurdle negative binomial model achieved the lowest AIC and BIC and reproduced the observed zero frequency. However, significant residual dispersion and uniformity tests indicated that some model misspecification remained. The hurdle model is therefore the preferred model in relative terms rather than evidence of perfect model adequacy.

Chronic conditions and self-perceived health were important predictors of the conditional count component. Holding other predictors constant, each additional chronic condition corresponded to approximately 15.9% higher modelled count intensity. Compared with average perceived health, poor health corresponded to approximately 46.6% higher intensity, whereas excellent health corresponded to approximately 30.8% lower intensity. Each additional year of education was associated with approximately 2.1% higher intensity, and private insurance with approximately 13.9% higher intensity. The gender contrast was not statistically significant at α = .05. These percentages are useful for interpreting the exponentiated coefficients, but the predictors use different measurement scales and cannot be ranked directly by the size of their coefficients. Moreover, these estimates refer to the model’s conditional count component and do not represent percentage changes in overall physician visits, which also depend on the hurdle’s zero component.

The chronic-conditions × insurance interaction was not statistically significant, suggesting that the relationship between chronic conditions and the conditional count component did not differ detectably between insurance groups. Nevertheless, insurance-group differences were observed at individual chronic-condition values. These findings are compatible because differences in predicted count levels can occur without significantly different slopes. The simple-effects results should therefore be interpreted as descriptions of estimated differences rather than evidence supporting moderation.

Several limitations should be considered. The study is observational, preventing causal conclusions about chronic conditions, health status, or insurance coverage. The data are also historically dated and may not represent current patterns of health-care utilization. Self-report and measurement error, unmeasured confounding, differences in health-care access, and socioeconomic factors may influence the observed associations. In addition, these analyses do not incorporate the complex survey design, and the final model continues to show some residual misspecification. Future analyses could evaluate additional predictors, flexible model specifications, and survey-design adjustments, followed by further residual checks.

plot_dharma_qq <- function(residual_object, heading) {

  n <- length(residual_object$scaledResiduals)

  qq_data <- tibble(
    Expected = ppoints(n),
    Observed = sort(residual_object$scaledResiduals)
  )

  ggplot(qq_data, aes(x = Expected, y = Observed)) +
    geom_abline(
      slope = 1,
      intercept = 0,
      linetype = "dashed",
      color = "#B85050",
      linewidth = 0.6
    ) +
    geom_point(
      size = 0.7,
      alpha = 0.45,
      color = "#32649A"
    ) +
    coord_equal(
      xlim = c(0, 1),
      ylim = c(0, 1),
      expand = FALSE
    ) +
    labs(
      title = heading,
      x = "Expected uniform quantiles",
      y = "Observed DHARMa quantiles"
    ) +
    theme_minimal(base_size = 12) +
    theme(
      plot.title = element_text(face = "bold", size = 13),
      plot.margin = margin(12, 15, 12, 12)
    )
}

### Interpretation of the graphs

The Poisson Q-Q plot shows a pronounced S-shaped departure from the reference diagonal. The residual-versus-fitted plot also reveals concentrations of observations near zero and one rather than an approximately uniform distribution.

Together, these patterns suggest that the Poisson model does not reproduce the observed count distribution adequately. They are consistent with the significant dispersion, zero-inflation, and uniformity tests.

### Why overdispersion matters

The Poisson model assumes that the conditional variance equals the conditional mean.

When the outcome is overdispersed, the model-based standard errors can be underestimated. This can produce confidence intervals that are too narrow and p-values that are overly optimistic, increasing the risk of incorrectly concluding that predictors are statistically significant.

### Why we should not immediately fit a zero-inflated model

Excess zeros do not necessarily indicate a separate structural-zero process.

Overdispersion can itself cause a Poisson model to underestimate the number of zeros. A negative binomial model allows additional variance and may therefore address both overdispersion and excess zeros without requiring an explicit zero-inflation component.

We first estimate a negative binomial model and reassess its diagnostics before deciding whether a separate zero process is necessary.

# Part 4: Negative Binomial Regression

## 4.1 Fit the NB2 model

The Poisson diagnostics indicated substantial overdispersion and many more zeros than predicted.

We therefore fit a negative binomial model (NB2), using the same five predictors and observations as M2.

The NB2 variance is **Var(Y \| X) = μ + μ²/θ**.

The additional dispersion parameter permits the variance to exceed the mean.

# Part 5: Do the Zeros Need Their Own Model?

## 5.1 Decide whether another zero model is justified

The NB2 model substantially improved the dispersion problem. Its dispersion ratio was `r format_num(unname(disp_nb$statistic))` (`r format_p(disp_nb$p.value)`).

However, its observed-to-expected zero ratio was `r format_num(unname(zi_nb$statistic))` (`r format_p(zi_nb$p.value)`).

`r if (zi_nb$p.value < .05 && unname(zi_nb$statistic) > 1) "The significant ratio above one indicates that NB2 still predicts too few zero visits, providing justification for comparing models with a separate zero component." else if (zi_nb$p.value >= .05) "The observed number of zeros is consistent with NB2 predictions, so an additional zero component is not required by this diagnostic." else "A significant discrepancy in the number of zeros remains, warranting further investigation."`

We consider two alternatives:

- **Zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB):** Zeros can arise from either the count-generating process or a separate structural-zero process.
- **Hurdle negative binomial:** A separate process models whether a person records any physician visits, while positive visit counts are modelled using a zero-truncated negative binomial distribution.

Both models include the same five predictors in their conditional count and zero components.

These models represent statistical processes and do not establish that particular individuals belong to a literal group that can never visit a physician.

# Part 6: Test and Probe an Interaction

## 6.1 Prediction

We test whether **private insurance (no versus yes)** moderates the association between chronic conditions and physician visits.

We predict that more chronic conditions will be associated with more office visits in both insurance groups, but the association may be stronger among participants with private insurance because coverage may reduce barriers to accessing medical care.

This is a statistical prediction, not a causal claim about the effect of insurance.

## 6.2 Fit the interaction model

We carry forward the **`r selected_name`** model selected in Part 5.

The interaction is added to the conditional count component as `chronic * insurance`, while all other predictors remain controls.

When the selected model includes a separate zero component, that component remains unchanged.

For the hurdle model, `component = "cond"` targets the log-linked negative binomial count component, whereas the `ggeffects` graph displays overall expected visits including zeros.

These are different quantities, so their predictions may differ.

Within the same additive conditional log-count component, the control-variable terms cancel when comparing two insurance levels at a fixed chronic-condition value. Consequently, the log-scale insurance contrast does not change merely because typical rather than averaged control values are used.

That invariance does not automatically extend to contrasts of the full two-part hurdle-model predictions.

### Paragraph 1 — Model selection

### Paragraph 2 — Main effects

## 7.2 Discussion

The findings demonstrate why count regression requires careful attention to the distribution of the outcome and the assumptions of the fitted model. Physician visits exhibited considerable variability, and the initial Poisson model substantially underestimated both dispersion and zeros. NB2 improved the representation of variability, but it continued to underpredict zeros. The hurdle negative binomial model achieved the lowest AIC and BIC and reproduced the observed zero frequency. However, significant residual dispersion and uniformity tests indicated that some model misspecification remained. The hurdle model is therefore the preferred model in relative terms rather than evidence of perfect model adequacy.

Chronic conditions and self-perceived health were important predictors of the conditional count component. Holding other predictors constant, each additional chronic condition corresponded to approximately `r format_num(pct_effect("chronic"), 1)`% higher modelled count intensity. Compared with average perceived health, poor health corresponded to approximately `r format_num(pct_effect("healthpoor"), 1)`% higher intensity, whereas excellent health corresponded to approximately `r format_num(abs(pct_effect("healthexcellent")), 1)`% lower intensity. Each additional year of education was associated with approximately `r format_num(pct_effect("school"), 1)`% higher intensity, and private insurance with approximately `r format_num(pct_effect("insuranceyes"), 1)`% higher intensity. The gender contrast was not statistically significant at α = .05. These percentages are useful for interpreting the exponentiated coefficients, but the predictors use different measurement scales and cannot be ranked directly by the size of their coefficients. Moreover, these estimates refer to the model's conditional count component and do not represent percentage changes in overall physician visits, which also depend on the hurdle's zero component.

The chronic-conditions × insurance interaction was not statistically significant, suggesting that the relationship between chronic conditions and the conditional count component did not differ detectably between insurance groups. Nevertheless, insurance-group differences were observed at individual chronic-condition values. These findings are compatible because differences in predicted count levels can occur without significantly different slopes. The simple-effects results should therefore be interpreted as descriptions of estimated differences rather than evidence supporting moderation.

Several limitations should be considered. The study is observational, preventing causal conclusions about chronic conditions, health status, or insurance coverage. The data are also historically dated and may not represent current patterns of health-care utilization. Self-report and measurement error, unmeasured confounding, differences in health-care access, and socioeconomic factors may influence the observed associations. In addition, these analyses do not incorporate the complex survey design, and the final model continues to show some residual misspecification. Future analyses could evaluate additional predictors, flexible model specifications, and survey-design adjustments, followed by further residual checks.
